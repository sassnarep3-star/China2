Getting CNY out of China as a U.S. citizen

A source-linked answer to one question: what is the least-tax, least-penalty way to turn a renminbi balance into dollars, and what does each route actually cost.

The short answer

There is no PRC tax on converting currency. The PRC individual income tax law taxes nine listed categories of income, and an exchange of your own renminbi is not one of them S05. What actually costs money, in descending order, is: (1) PRC tax on the underlying income if it was never paid, (2) the bank's exchange spread, (3) fixed bank and correspondent fees, and (4) U.S. reporting penalties if the account was never declared. Of these, only the spread and the fixed fees are costs of converting; the rest are costs of the history of the money.

So the question to answer first is not "how do I convert", it is "what is this balance, and can I document it?" A documented, already-taxed balance moves out at roughly 1,763.32 on USD 400,000 (0.441%) in fees and spread. An undocumented balance has no lawful route at any price.

The seven things that decide the answer

  1. Your characterisation in PRC FX law. A US citizen living in China for a continuous year or more is a domestic individual; otherwise a non-domestic individual S05 Art. 52(2). The operative rule differs: a domestic individual's purchases of FX face the annual total S02 Art. 2, while a non-domestic individual converting lawful current-account RMB proceeds on identity plus source and tax documents SAFE 2026 guidance.
  2. The USD 50,000 figure is narrower than it looks. It is an annual total for individuals' FX sales and for a domestic individual's FX purchases S02 Art. 2. Above it, the transaction is not banned: it moves to a documents-based review S02 Arts. 10-12. So "my money is stuck because of the 50,000 cap" is usually a documents problem wearing a quota's clothes.
  3. Using other people's quotas is a separate offence. Borrowing a quota, or lending yours, puts you on SAFE's watch list for that year plus two more S09 point 2, and splitting to evade limits is expressly prohibited S01 Art. 7. This closes the most commonly suggested workaround.
  4. The U.S. side has a real but narrow tax. Disposing of foreign currency is a section 988 transaction 26 USC 988(c)(1)(C)(i), but for an individual's personal transaction no gain is recognised unless it exceeds USD 200 26 USC 988(e)(2), restated in IRS Publication 525. At IRS yearly averages from 2021 to 2025 the renminbi moved from 6.452 to 7.129 per dollar — a loss for anyone who acquired RMB in 2021-2023 IRS yearly averages. See Tax & penalties for the arithmetic.
  5. Reporting is due whether or not you move anything. A foreign account aggregate over USD 10,000 must be reported on the FBAR IRS FBAR page and a balance over the 8938 thresholds on Form 8938 26 USC 6038D. The FBAR penalty maximums are USD 16,536 non-willful and USD 165,353 willful per violation 31 CFR 1010.821. This is the largest number in this whole project and it has nothing to do with converting.
  6. Cash is capped twice. Out of China: USD 5,000 without a permit, USD 10,000 with one, and in principle no more above that SAFE/customs 2003. Onto a card abroad: CNY 100,000 equivalent per person per calendar year, with a two-year suspension for exceeding it SAFE 2017. Neither can move a six-figure balance.
  7. The published spread is a board rate, not your quote. The one same-day published retail figure verified here is Bank of China's, 665.72 buy against 668.52 sell per 100 USD, a 0.419% spread BOC board rates, on a page that says it is for reference only. Every cost table below uses it as a labelled assumption. Replace it with the bank's executable quote before acting.

Read this before you trust any number on this site

  • The single biggest unknown is whether your balance is documented. Nothing published lets an undocumented balance leave lawfully. The tax and the permit are on the money's history, not on the exchange.
  • The spread is an assumption. It is one bank's published board rate from 10 Nov 2026 S22, applied uniformly. Your bank's executable rate will differ, and it moves the total far more than any fixed fee.
  • Fixed fees are one bank's published tariff. HSBC China, effective 31 October 2026 S11. Other banks publish different schedules, and the tariff itself excludes intermediary and receiving-bank charges S11 Important Note 7.
  • Nothing here knows your facts. Tax residency days, household registration status, how the balance was earned, whether PRC tax was withheld, and whether the FBAR was ever filed are all unknown. Where a fact is unknown the cell is blank and the item is on Flagged for Review.
  • Correction to earlier material in this repository. Some earlier pages in this repo quoted a "USD 0-7" same-name Global Transfer fee and a "USD 7" reference. In the current tariff the USD 7 figure belongs to International Education Payment (item 2.2.7), and same-name Global Transfer is Free for Premier and Private Banking S11 items 2.2.6.1 and 2.2.7. The guide uses the current text.

How to read the cost tables

The ladder prices each method at each USD 10,000 step from 10,000 to 400,000. Three columns are kept separate on purpose, so you can recompute every cell by hand:

  • Published fee = clamp(0.1% × principal, CNY100/7.10, CNY500/7.10) + CNY120/7.10 for the standard wire S11 2.2.4.1-2.2.4.2; zero for the items published Free S11 2.2.6.1.
  • Spread = principal × 0.419%, the labelled assumption from S22.
  • Total = published fee + spread. It excludes intermediary and receiving-bank charges, which the tariff itself excludes S11 Important Note 7, and excludes any personal tax.

Tiers are USD amounts, so no exchange-rate forecast is embedded in the model. The 7.10 CNY/USD rate is used only to express CNY-denominated published fees in dollars; at USD 400,000 the whole effect of that choice on a capped fee is under USD 15.
