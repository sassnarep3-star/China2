Tax and penalties: what actually attaches to the money

Three different things get confused in this conversation: a tax on income, a tax on a currency gain, and a penalty for not reporting. Only the first two are taxes. Below, each is tied to the statute or regulation that actually says it.

1. China: there is no tax on converting

The Individual Income Tax Law taxes nine enumerated categories of income: wages and salaries; remuneration for labour services; author's remuneration; royalties; business income; interest, dividends and bonuses; property leasing; property transfer; and incidental income. Disposing of your own renminbi is not one of them. Rates run from 3% to 45% on comprehensive income, 5% to 35% on business income, and a flat 20% on interest, dividends, property leasing, property transfer and incidental income S19.

This does not mean the balance was never taxable. If the money is unpaid remuneration or an unreported gain, the PRC tax attaches to the income, and the bank is required to review the real, lawful transaction basis before it sells you foreign exchange S01. That is the distinction the brief's "conversion penalty" most likely refers to (G-03).

2. United States: sec. 988 and the $200 line

A personal conversion of non-functional currency is a section 988 transaction, but for an individual's personal transaction no gain is recognised by reason of exchange-rate changes unless the gain would exceed $200. Above that line the exclusion does not apply and the gain is ordinary income. "Personal transaction" excludes anything whose allocable expenses meet section 162 or 212, so business or investment activity can fall outside the exclusion S06.

Income is translated into dollars when it is received, paid or accrued, at the spot rate; the IRS has no official rate and accepts any posted rate used consistently S05. So a later conversion cannot create income twice — at most it creates an exchange difference between the acquisition date and the conversion date.

5. The real penalty exposure is reporting, not conversion

A foreign account holding over $10,000 at any time in the year must be reported on an FBAR even if it produced no income. The current inflation-adjusted per-violation maxima are $16,536 non-willful and $165,353 willful; the general willful BSA range is $71,545 to $286,184 S08 S09. Those are maximums, asserted on facts and circumstances — not typical amounts. Compare them with the $86.97 published wire fee on $400,000: the compliance question is orders of magnitude larger than the transfer cost.

Form 8938 is a separate obligation with its own thresholds S10. Bringing more than $10,000 in cash into the U.S. requires a FinCEN Form 105 S13.
