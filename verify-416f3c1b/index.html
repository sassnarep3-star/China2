Getting CNY out of China: what is verified, what it costs, and what is still unknown

A U.S. citizen with money in a mainland China bank account does not face a special "conversion tax". The PRC Individual Income Tax Law taxes nine listed categories of income, and turning your own renminbi into dollars is not one of them. The real constraints are three: whether the balance can be documented, what the bank charges and spreads, and whether your U.S. reporting is clean. This page separates those three and leaves every unverifiable number blank.

The short answer

  1. Do not try to avoid a tax on converting. Both tax systems attach to income, not to the exchange itself. The "considerable tax" you were quoted is most likely one of: an under-documented source of funds, a Chinese income tax liability on the money when earned, a bank refusal dressed up as a tax, or a U.S. reporting exposure that has nothing to do with the transfer.
  2. Fix the paper first. If the balance came from lawful employment in China, the route is a bank purchase of foreign exchange against documented current-account income, with tax vouchers. That route has no published USD ceiling; it is limited by evidence, not by a number S02 S03.
  3. The fee is not the problem. The published wire fee at $86.97 on a USD 400,000-equivalent transfer is trivial. The unpublished FX spread and the penalty exposure are where the money actually goes.
  4. Never use crypto, underground banks or quota splitting. All three are prohibited and the "saving" is the price of an unlawful transaction S23 S20 S01.

Which of the lawful routes applies to you depends on facts that were not supplied: your residency, whether you ever held Chinese household registration, the origin and dates of the balance, and your U.S. filing history. Those are listed openly on Flagged for review rather than guessed.
