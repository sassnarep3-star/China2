Breaking down the conversion penalty

The premise that converting CNY to USD necessarily costs a lot is true for some money and false for others. This page decomposes the penalty into five separable layers, because the cheapest solution depends on which layer you are actually paying.

Layer 1 — Was the money already taxed in China?

This is the single most important question, and it determines almost everything else.

Key finding

If the balance is personal, lawfully-earned, already-taxed money in a personal account, there is no Chinese tax on converting it at all. The only Chinese-side cost is the bank's FX spread. The "considerable amount of taxes" is almost certainly either company-level EIT and WHT that have not yet been paid, or the US-side currency gain under § 988(e), or a professional's estimate that assumed the money is untaxed corporate profit.

The US–China treaty halves the withholding rate on every passive income stream and cuts equipment rental to an effective 7%. Not claiming treaty benefits — which banks will not always do automatically — costs 10 percentage points. This is the largest easily-avoidable leakage in the whole exercise.

Layer 4 — The US currency gain, § 988(e)

RuleEffectSource
§ 988(e)(1)The section does not apply to an individual's personal transactions§ 988
§ 988(e)(2)No gain recognised on exchange-rate movement in a personal transaction unless the gain exceeds USD 200 — then the entire gain is taxable ordinary income§ 988
This is the hidden penalty

Two points matter. One: the USD 200 threshold is a cliff, not a de minimis allowance — cross it by one dollar and the whole gain is in income. Two: there is no Chinese tax credit available against it, because China does not tax a currency gain. For someone who acquired CNY years ago at a strong rate and converts today, this can be a double-digit percentage of the balance, with no offsetting credit. It is invisible unless you specifically check § 988(e) — and it is often the real reason a large conversion "costs a lot".

Layer 5 dwarfs layers 1–4

The downside of doing this wrong is an order of magnitude larger than the upside of doing it cheaply. A willful FBAR penalty alone — the greater of USD 165,353 or 50% of the balance — exceeds any tax saving available from any method in this catalogue. Optimising tax while risking a penalty is the wrong trade.

What actually drives the answer

  1. Is the money already taxed in China? If yes, the conversion itself is essentially tax-free in China and the only leakage is the US § 988(e) gain and the bank spread.
  2. Is the money company profit? If yes, the leakage is 32.5% — but claim the treaty (10%, not 20%) and the foreign tax credit, and the net US cost is usually zero.
  3. Do you have genuine overseas obligations? Paying them directly from the CNY account is the only route that is tax-free on both sides.
  4. Are you still employed in China and qualifying for the FEIE? If yes, salary remittance is tax-free on both sides up to USD 130,000 (2025).
  5. Are you emigrating or leaving China? The personal-property-transfer channel exists for exactly this — get it verified before using it.
  6. Never use underground banking, crypto, splitting, or borrowed quotas. The criminal and penalty exposure dwarfs any tax saving.
