Taxes on both sides

China taxes income at 3–45% or a flat 20%; the US taxes worldwide income but not the transfer — and neither taxes the act of converting.

The tax analysis

China taxes income. The US taxes worldwide income of citizens. Neither taxes the act of changing currency. The word "penalty" in the brief is doing a lot of work — most of it is a bank margin, and the rest is income tax on money that was never declared in the first place.

China: why conversion is not a taxable event

The PRC Individual Income Tax Law lists nine income categories and taxes nothing else. A conversion between currencies is not among them.

Currency for tax purposes

IIT is computed in RMB. Foreign-currency income is converted at the PBOC central parity P11 — the same rate series this site uses to turn published CNY fees into dollars P14.

The United States: three separate questions

QuestionRuleSource
Is the transfer income? No. A US citizen is taxed on worldwide income; moving your own money is not income. U08
Is the currency itself taxed? Only on a gain over US$200. §988 makes FX gain ordinary, not capital — but a personal transaction is excluded unless the gain exceeds US$200. U05
Is there double taxation of the Chinese income? Relieved by the foreign tax credit — a credit, not a deduction, on Form 1116. Not available for tax on income you excluded under the FEIE, and only the treaty-reduced amount is creditable. U06 U07

The treaty, and why it does not shelter a US citizen

The US–PRC income tax convention (signed 1984, in force 1987) caps source-country tax on dividends, interest and royalties at 10% of the gross amount U07. But its saving clause says the United States may tax its citizens notwithstanding any provision of the agreement U10. Practical effect: the treaty limits China's claims and shapes what you may credit — it does not reduce your US filing obligation.

The honest bottom line

For a US citizen moving taxed salary out of China, the combined tax bill on the conversion itself is zero on both sides. What you actually pay is a bank wire tariff (cents on the dollar), an unpublished FX spread (the real cost), and — if the money was never taxed in China — the Chinese income tax you already owed. The expensive mistakes are reporting failures and prohibited routes, and both are avoidable.
