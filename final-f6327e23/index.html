Getting CNY out of China legally, at least cost

A US citizen's question, answered from primary sources: what actually costs money, what is actually taxed, and what the rules actually cap.

The conversion itself is not a taxable event, and the famous USD 50,000 limit is not a cap on converting documented current-account income. The real cost of the lawful route is small — roughly 0.70% at $10,000 falling to 0.24% at $400,000 — and the real risk is US reporting, not a Chinese conversion levy.

1 · There is no Chinese tax on the conversion

The FX Regulations state that "the State does not restrict current international payments and transfers" (Art. 5) S02. The conversion rule requires the bank to see your ID and the source-of-funds papers including tax vouchers S01 — the tax attaches to the income, which is assessed separately, not to the act of changing currency.

Analytical conclusion. No provision imposing a conversion levy appears in the texts I read; that is not a regulator's positive statement. See flag F06.

2 · The USD 50,000 quota is a different channel

Art. 54 imposes the USD 50,000 annual facilitation quota on individuals' FX sales and on domestic individuals' purchases. Art. 59 handles a non-domestic individual's documented current-account income with no quota stated S01 S03. A PRC permanent-resident ID holder may use the quota instead.

Watch the wording trap: "no quota" is not "no documents". The bank decides.

3 · What the transfer actually costs

Bank fee + cable + FX spread + your US bank's incoming fee. At $100,000: bank fee $92.74, spread $209.86, receiving $16 → $318.60 all-in S14 S15 S16.

Fee schedule is HSBC China's; your bank's will differ.

4 · Where the real tax risk sits

US side: a personal conversion with more than $200 of exchange gain falls outside the personal-transaction exclusion S08; the account itself triggers FBAR above $10,000 S10 and possibly Form 8938 S11. Penalty maxima are published and large S09.

Gain depends on when you acquired the CNY — a private fact.
