Tax & reporting

Two tax systems, neither of which taxes the exchange

China taxes the income that produced the renminbi; the United States taxes the currency gain on disposal and requires the account to be reported. Neither reviewed instrument taxes the act of converting. What follows is the statutory framework with its thresholds, and one illustration — clearly labelled — because your basis, residency and other income are unknown.

The one illustration, and why it is only an illustration

A currency gain needs a dollar basis: the USD value of the renminbi when each tranche was received, compared with the USD received on conversion. That is unknown here (Q05). What can be shown is the statutory rate that would apply to a given ordinary gain for a single filer with no other taxable income, using the 2026 brackets.

Brackets and standard deduction from Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §4.01 Table 3 and §4.14 (S22). The 2026 single standard deduction of $16,100 is applied to the gain only; other income would push it into higher brackets, and the FEIE or a treaty position could change the result. The $6,226.68 row uses the IRS yearly averages of 7.189 and 7.129 (S21) purely to size the gain — it is not a basis you actually have.

The asymmetry worth noticing. Every cost in the conversion itself is measured in hundreds of dollars. The U.S. reporting maxima in the same table are measured in tens and hundreds of thousands. If the FBAR and 8938 history is unclear, that — not the spread — is the number to resolve before moving funds.

What this is. A document review. No bank was contacted, no account accessed, no funds moved, no return filed. No personal tax figure is asserted: basis, residency, filing status and the source of the balance are unknown. Every computed figure is arithmetic on a rate or fee the publisher printed, and every published rate board says it is not the executable price — so all totals are lower bounds.
